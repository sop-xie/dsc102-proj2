Proposition: Male officers face more consequences for misconduct than female officers

Data. Both charts use the NYC Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) records that ProPublica released in July 2020: 33,358 allegations against 3,996 NYPD officers (3,609 men and 387 women), filed between 1985 and 2020. ProPublica included only officers who were still on the force in June 2020 and who had at least one substantiated allegation. On the charts, an authorized complaint is a substantiated allegation, meaning the board found that the officer broke NYPD rules. The harshest punishment is the board's recommendation that the officer face a disciplinary trial ("Substantiated (Charges)"). The board only recommends discipline; the police commissioner makes the final decision.

How to read the scores

Each design decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest): negative scores push readers toward a conclusion the data doesn't fully support, and positive scores help them see what it actually shows.

FOR the Proposition

Data transformations. We used pandas in Python. The dataset has one row per allegation: 33,358 allegations from 12,056 complaints against 3,996 officers, with no missing values in the columns we used.

  1. Flagged each allegation. An allegation is authorized if its board_disposition starts with "Substantiated" (8,301 allegations, across all nine substantiated categories). It counts as the harshest punishment only if it is exactly "Substantiated (Charges)" (3,796 allegations).
  2. Grouped by officer. We grouped allegations by unique_mos_id and counted each officer's authorized allegations and harshest punishments, giving one row per officer (3,609 men and 387 women). Each officer has a single gender in the data.
  3. Averaged by gender. Men average 2.12 authorized allegations and 0.99 harshest punishments per officer; women average 1.64 and 0.58. The chart rounds these to one decimal.
  4. Computed the callouts. Each callout is men's average divided by women's, minus one: 2.12 ÷ 1.64 → 30% more, and 0.99 ÷ 0.58 → 69% more.

One complaint can contain several allegations, sometimes against different officers. The chart's "authorized complaints" therefore counts substantiated allegations, not whole complaints. We kept "complaints" in the label because it is easier to read.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • -1.5 Counting per officer instead of per complaint. Men collect more punishments partly because they have about twice as many allegations each (8.8 vs. 4.5). Dividing by allegations would show that a smaller share of men's complaints are authorized (24% vs. 36%), which is the opposite story. An earnest version would add allegations per officer as a third pair of bars, which would explain much of the gap. Every number here is true, and the missing denominator does the persuading.
  • -1 Loaded wording in the title and callouts. The title says men receive "harsher punishments," but the chart shows that men receive more of the harshest punishment, not that their punishments are harsher. We never measured severity. An earnest title would be "Male officers receive more complaints and more of the harshest punishments." The callouts, "Men: 30% more complaints" and "69% more harshest punishments," are accurate ratios, but the absolute gaps are only 0.5 and 0.4 per officer. In our first draft the callouts floated above the men's bars. We added brackets joining each pair so the callout reads as the size of the gap, and put women on the left so the eye steps up to the taller men's bar.
  • +1.5 Simple grouped bars on a zero baseline. This chart changed form more than any other. We started with grouped bars, tried a horizontal dumbbell chart (two dots per row on a shared scale), then a vertical lollipop chart, and went back to bars because the lollipops were hard to read. We then made the bars thinner, moved the legend into the empty top-right corner, and added a y-axis title ("Average per officer"), because bare numbers like 2.1 were ambiguous without it. Bars on a zero baseline are the easiest form to compare accurately, and the source line names the dataset and the "at least one authorized complaint" filter.
  • +0.5 Plain-language terms, with the originals underneath. The dataset's terms, "substantiated allegation" and "Substantiated (Charges)," confused our own group, so we replaced them. We first used "confirmed complaint," then switched to "authorized complaint" for every chart and caption. "Authorized" is shorter, but it can read as "approved," which is not what substantiated means, so we show the dataset's terms in small gray text under each label for readers who want to check. "Harshest punishment" carries an asterisk and a footnote saying we only count the top punishment level. The footnote simplifies, because the board uses more than three categories. These labels make the chart readable, and they also sound more decisive than the originals.
  • -0.5 Color and police-themed symbols. The palette went from default blue and orange, to fall rust and gold, to navy for men and pink for women. We checked that navy and pink stay distinct under every type of color blindness, and because pink has low contrast on white, every number is printed in dark ink rather than in the bar color. We then added a police theme: DIN Condensed, a signage-style typeface, for titles and numbers, a shield icon with an "NYPD · CIVILIAN COMPLAINTS" line above the title, and officer-in-cap icons in place of plain legend squares. Navy and pink let readers identify each gender instantly, but they lean on a stereotype. The police styling makes the chart look official, even though it did not come from the NYPD, and that borrowed authority makes the framing more believable.

AGAINST the Proposition

Data transformations. The dataset doesn't record promotions, so we inferred them from the two rank columns, using the same 3,996 officers as Figure 1.

  1. Ranked the ranks. We mapped the eight NYPD ranks to seven levels: Police Officer (0), Sergeant and Detective (1), Lieutenant (2), Captain (3), Deputy Inspector (4), Inspector (5), and Chiefs and other ranks (6). Sergeant and Detective share a level, so moving between them is not a promotion.
  2. Flagged promotions. An allegation is followed by a promotion if the officer's level in rank_now (their rank in 2020) is higher than in rank_incident (their rank at the time of the allegation).
  3. Grouped by officer. An officer counts as promoted if any of their allegations was followed by a promotion, and as having got the harshest punishment if any allegation was "Substantiated (Charges)". This splits officers into four groups: men who got it (1,613), women who got it (135), men who never did (1,996), and women who never did (252).
  4. Computed promotion rates. For each group, we divided promoted officers by all officers: 1,073 of 1,613 men (66.5%) and 60 of 135 women (44.4%) who got the harshest punishment; 811 of 1,996 men (40.6%) and 78 of 252 women (31.0%) who never did.
  5. Computed the gaps. We rounded each rate to a whole percent before subtracting, so the labeled gaps match the printed bars: 67 − 44 = 23 and 41 − 31 = 10. The unrounded gaps are 22.1 and 9.7 percentage points.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • -1 "Rewarded" in the title. The title frames promotion as a reward for misconduct. The data only shows that harsh punishment and promotion go together. Officers with longer careers collect both more complaints and more promotions, so tenure likely explains much of the pattern. The title went through four versions. The first, "Harshly punished men were promoted twice as often as women who weren't," compared men who got the harshest punishment (67%) with women who never did (31%), the most extreme pairing on the chart and our most deceptive draft. We then compared like with like, "men were promoted 50% more often than women" (67% vs. 44%), then "The promotion gap between men and women is widest after the harshest punishment," which was the most accurate. We settled on "rewarded" because it is the most striking.
  • +1 Grouped bars, after trying several other forms. We started with a slope chart and dropped it, because lines connecting the two groups suggested change over time. We then tried promoted vs. not promoted bars, an icon array of 10 officers per row, pie charts, a rank pyramid, overlaid bars, and one bar per gender split by shading. The icon array rounded 67% and 44% to 7 and 4 icons, which made a 50% gap look like 75%. The single bars looked stacked, as if the dark top section were a separate number. Grouped bars on a zero baseline are the easiest to compare. The y-axis title, "% of officers promoted," and the subtitle, which defines the harshest punishment as a recommendation for a disciplinary trial, make clear that each number is a share of officers.
  • -0.5 Gap brackets instead of a ratio. We replaced the "50% more often" ratio with brackets labeled "10-percent gap" and "23-percent gap," with dotted guide lines from each bar top, so readers compare the two gaps directly. That is more honest than the ratio. However, the labels say "percent" when they are percentage points, and the 23 comes from rounded values (the exact gap is 22.1). We also tried a dumbbell chart that drew each gap as a line. We went back to grouped bars, which also show each group's promotion rate as a bar height.
  • +0.5 Fading the "never got it" pair, and marking the key term. The left pair is drawn at 75% opacity, so the eye lands on the "got it" pair while the comparison stays readable. We tried 35%, 75%, and 57.5% before settling on 75%. Lighter versions pushed the context into the background, and in the overlaid-bar draft, see-through pink over see-through navy blended into purple. Keeping the pair visible lets readers see the gap grow, rather than seeing only the headline pair. Both "harshest punishment" labels carry the same asterisk and footnote as Figure 1, and the same officer icons mark each gender.
  • -1 Not showing who is missing. The data only includes officers still on the force in June 2020. Officers who were fired or pushed out after serious discipline are missing, which raises the promotion rate of the punished group. We also don't know when or why anyone was promoted. None of this appears on the chart, which presents inferred promotion as if it were a recorded outcome.

Final Reflection

What surprised us most was that both sides came from the same honest data, with every axis starting at zero. The choice of denominator did most of the work. Counting punishments per officer makes men look more punished, because they have about twice as many allegations. Dividing by allegations, or looking at what happened to officers afterward, makes men look like they get away with more. The data side was straightforward once we built one row per officer. The hard part was wording. We revised the titles and labels more than the charts themselves, going back and forth on "more" versus "harsher," "received" versus "recommended for," and "percent" versus "percentage points." Each of those words changed the message. We also had to be careful with women's numbers, since only 387 of the 3,996 officers are women.

We define ethical analysis and visualization as work where a careful reader can recover the truth from what is shown and disclosed. Every number should be accurate, and every encoding should be proportional to the data. Choices about which measure to show and who is included should be visible on the chart, not hidden in the code. The more general the audience, the higher the bar, because fewer readers will check the small print.

We think persuasion is acceptable when it picks which true measure to emphasize, labels that measure clearly, and keeps the scales honest. Titles that state a conclusion and color or opacity that directs attention fall in this category. Design becomes misleading when it leads readers to believe something the data doesn't support. That includes words that imply cause, like "rewarded," or severity, like "harsher." It also includes calling percentage points "percent," and hiding a denominator the conclusion depends on. Our official-looking police styling falls close to the line too, because the charts did not come from the NYPD. We drew our own line at distorting the data itself. We kept every axis at zero, dropped an early draft with an axis zoomed to 30–50%, and dropped an icon array whose rounding inflated a gap. Looking back, some of the framing we allowed, such as "rewarded," crosses that line, because it asserts a cause the data cannot show.
